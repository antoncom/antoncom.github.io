About this document

This document sets out a management model that the author derived from the works of A.S. Makarenko and tested in practice: in 2008–2012 in his own web studio, and since 2022 in a distributed R&D team at an industrial enterprise. It records what has already been achieved and proposes a programme for developing the model further — as a foundation for the work of a manager (team lead, head of department, technical director, organisational development manager).

The document is also a summary of five years of work on the “Wireless LTE/4G Router” start-up, and an expression of gratitude to the management of Rostov Electronics Plant LLC for the trust, patience and support without which this experience would not have been possible.

1. Brief historical background

Anton Semyonovich Makarenko (1888–1939) went down in history as an educator, but in the 1920s and 1930s he effectively did the job of a director: he built a team from scratch, set up production, and was responsible for finances and results. His experience is valuable to managers because it was tested not in a classroom but in the economy — at working enterprises.

The Gorky Colony (1920–1928)

In September 1920 Makarenko took on the creation and management of an institution for the re-education of adolescents near Poltava — it was later named the Maxim Gorky Labour Colony. From 1923 the colony became an experimental model institution of the People's Commissariat of Education of the Ukrainian SSR, and in the spring of 1926 it was moved to Kuryazh near Kharkiv.

It was here that the main tools of the system took shape: detachments and commanders, the council of commanders, composite detachments, and the general assembly as the collective's highest body. Makarenko later described this experience in “The Pedagogical Poem” (1935).

The Dzerzhinsky Commune (1927–1935)

The F.E. Dzerzhinsky Children's Labour Commune was founded in the settlement of Novy Kharkov, funded by voluntary deductions from the salaries of Ukraine's security officers, and formally opened on 29 December 1927. Makarenko was invited to run it; the core of the collective consisted of former pupils of the Gorky Colony. Makarenko headed the commune until 1935.

The commune's key distinguishing feature was the combination of learning with productive work: first in workshops, then in real factory shops. In 1930 a workers' faculty of the Kharkiv Machine-Building Institute opened at the commune, and in 1934 a ten-year school.

The commune's factories: FD-1 and FED

  • 1927 — the commune opens; small workshops.
  • 1930 — the first production shops are launched.
  • January 1932 — an electric-tool factory opens at the commune; it gave the country the first Soviet electric drill, the FD-1, which was in very short supply at the time.
  • 1932–1934 — production of a small-format film camera is mastered. The first batch of FED cameras (the initials of F.E. Dzerzhinsky) came out in 1933, with serial production from 1934; it was the first Soviet portable film camera.
  • 1938 — the commune's two factories are merged into a single industrial complex; in 1941 it was transferred to the aviation industry.

FED cameras were produced in series until about 1996, and the enterprise that grew out of the commune's workshops still exists in Kharkiv today.

Results: what was achieved

Self-sufficiency

The commune not only supported itself but also set up the production of cameras and power tools; according to estimates cited in the literature, it contributed about 4.5 million roubles a year to the state.

High-tech product

A collective recruited from homeless adolescents mastered precision instrument-making within a few years — a product on a par with the best European models.

People

The workers' faculty and the ten-year school turned production into a school: the communards worked, studied and prepared for university at the same time.

Method

The principle of the unity of respect and demands was formulated and tested in practice, together with a system of collective self-government.

Makarenko's books “March of the Year '30” (1932), “FD-1” and “Flags on the Battlements” (1938) are devoted to the work of the commune.

2. Brief overview of the model

The main principle

“As many demands on a person as possible, and as much respect for them as possible.”

This is not a compromise between softness and strictness but a single statement: high demands on a person are a form of respect for them, while respect without demands is a form of indifference. Hence the formula we use in the team: max(R) = max(D) — maximum respect with maximum demands. A demand becomes respect only when it is achievable and clear.

Elements of the system

ElementEssence
The collective as a toolA person develops in and through the collective. The leader works not with each person separately but with the collective, which itself makes demands on its members.
Productive workWork is not “occupational therapy” but a real business with a plan, quality, cost and responsibility to the customer.
Detachments organised by productionThe primary collective is the detachment, which is also a unit in production. In a mature collective there are mixed-age detachments where the older teach the younger.
Self-governmentThe council of commanders and the general assembly; composite detachments for specific tasks lasting 1–2 weeks with temporary commanders; duty rotas and alternating roles of leading and following.
Lines of perspectiveEach person and the collective have a visible “tomorrow's joy”: near, medium and long-term prospects — growth, new production, a new level.
Parallel actionThe leader influences a person through the collective rather than through direct pressure; public assessment of work carries more weight than private.
Traditions and styleA stable way of life — routine, rituals, reports, shared outings — is the main factor of upbringing. The rules are written down and the same for everyone.
A collective of educatorsYou cannot bring up a collective of workers without a united collective of leaders: honest, fair, educated, free of envy and of “chasing their subordinates' affection”.

Why it applies to adults

Makarenko worked with adolescents, and applying his model to adult professionals requires a caveat. What we take from it is not “upbringing” but the structure of the collective: transparency, clear rules, the same demands for everyone, prospects for growth and the passing of experience from senior to junior.

The strength of the model depends on the employee's level: for interns and juniors it is above all a school; for middles and seniors it is partnership and autonomy.

Re-educating an adult collective with established habits is much harder than forming a new one — which is why the model takes root best in new or separate teams.

3. How it works 100 years later

A hundred years on, conditions are radically different: instead of a commune on the outskirts of Kharkiv there is a distributed R&D team whose engineers live in different cities and time zones and may not see each other in person for years. All the more remarkable that Makarenko's mechanisms carry over almost one-to-one — only the tools change.

What this means for a director

  • Manage the way of life, not people one by one. The director designs rules, rhythm and traditions; after that the collective upholds the demands itself. Written policies work even when the director is not around.
  • Trust through transparency. A remote employee cannot be “watched over their shoulder”. A report visible to the whole team replaces supervision and removes conflicts of the “what am I paying you for” kind.
  • Demands on results, respect for the person. A deadline missed for an objective reason is no reproach if the reports were kept honestly; failing to warn in time is.
  • Predictable money. Pay growth by a single formula and automatic indexation remove haggling and the dependence of salary on the ability to ask.
  • Prospects for everyone. An intern sees the path to junior, a junior to middle; a mentor is not afraid to train a replacement, because there is always room for a step forward.
  • Measurability. TeamTime BPM turns rules into data: time logged, reports, fulfilment of monthly commitments.

4. Policies for teamwork

Below are the team policies currently in force or approved at the enterprise (2026). The first group is the team regulations (operational rules, max(D)); the second is pay and employee programmes (expressions of max(R)). Each policy is given in summary with a link to the full text.

Corporate Culture Policy: max(R) = max(D) in force

The culture of working relations is described in a single phrase: “Maximum respect with maximum demands”. In new situations not covered by the regulations, everyone is guided by this principle.

max(R) — what the enterprise owes its employees

1. The people who raise the money take responsibility before investors, secure guarantees and put their own property at risk in order to pay salaries on time.
2. The authors of technical specifications make sure a task has a technical solution and do not demand the impossible.
3. Mentors share their experience without fear of losing their place: training a replacement is respect for the enterprise.
4. Seniors, middles, juniors: after a year of work a move to the next level is possible — respect for personal ambitions.
5. Interns: nobody is “put on probation” — newcomers are invited to get acquainted, and together we decide whether we can be useful to each other.

Why the demands are strict

The strict wording of the policies was earned through experience and is itself a form of respect. The enterprise pays for all the actual time during which an employee is consciously engaged in work, including experiments, testing hypotheses and fixing mistakes.

No one has to work at the pace of the fastest senior — what matters is a real interest in a high-quality result and reliability (once you give your word, keep it). In return only one thing is required: that this working time is visible.

Six Sun Hours (SSH) Policy in force

  • The team works in daylight. Those who cannot shift their routine to daylight hours are not a fit for this collaboration.
  • Full-time: at least 6 working hours during daytime in your own time zone. A weekday without a single report in TeamTime counts as unpaid leave.
  • Part-time and interns: the agreed number of actual hours must be logged in TeamTime each week. A week without reports counts as unpaid leave.

Daily Reports Policy in force

Most employees work remotely, and the work is built on trust: neither the team lead nor the director has any wish to stand over anyone's shoulder. Mutual understanding is achieved by everyone regularly and briefly reporting what is happening in their area — that is the report.

A report is a brief, precise, well-founded statement of what you did, how and why, and how much time it took.
Typical mistakeThe right way
No completed action (“analysing”)Use verbs of completed action: “analysed”, “did”.
InconsistencyWhat I did → what facts emerged → what I plan to do next.

A two-week “scroll”Report every 45, 90, 180 or 360 minutes of mental work; a manager has 3–5 minutes to read — show completed results briefly.

“Chat Time 15:00” Policy in force

  • On weekdays from 15:00 to 17:00 Moscow time employees ask each other work questions in personal chats and video calls.
  • Outside this window everyone has the right not to reply, protecting their focus and remembering their promise to complete the monthly plan.
  • Time spent on communication is logged in TeamTime with a brief summary of the problem discussed.
  • Presence during chat time: mandatory for full-time staff, desirable for interns and part-timers.

Full text: Chat Time 15:00

“Rhythm” Meetings Policy in force

The “Rhythm” reduces the number of reasons to distract colleagues during the working week: the more responsible the position, the more valuable the person's attention.

  • Questions are divided into urgent and non-urgent. Non-urgent ones are written down and brought to the “Rhythm”.
  • Before an urgent question, ask yourself: “Will Google/AI answer this for me?” If not, prepare half the answer — better still, options for a solution — and ask only which one to choose.
  • The “Rhythm” is held every two weeks, exactly 20 minutes: one minute for the essence of a question, 1–2 minutes for the answer; complex topics are taken outside the meeting.
  • A secretary is appointed for each “Rhythm”: collects the agenda in advance and writes a summary in their report afterwards.

Full text: “Rhythm” Meetings

Monthly Work Plan Policy in force

  • At the start of the month the list of tasks is discussed at the “Rhythm”; everyone answers whether they are ready to promise to complete the plan by the end of the month.
  • This promise is as binding as paying salaries on time.
  • The project manager must prepare detailed assignments for everyone by the start of the month.
  • A missed deadline for an objective reason (unrealistic deadlines, unforeseen technical difficulties) is no reproach if the reports were kept carefully and without idle time.
  • A missed deadline for a subjective reason (personal circumstances) is no reproach if it was reported in good time.

TeamTime Acceleration applied when hiring

A pay system in which salary grows steadily with experience in the specialty, confirmed by daily reports. The goal is to make growth predictable and transparent.

  • Pay = base salary (a fixed part that is never reduced) + piece-rate (work actually done, confirmed by reports with time).
  • The piece-rate is calculated at the hourly rate in the contract; the hourly rate is set by a single formula for everyone.
hRate = Rbase × Kedu × Ktenure

Rbase   = Rlevel + (Rnext − Rlevel) × (skillHours − Hlevel) / (Hnext − Hlevel)
Kedu    = 1 + 0.02 × eduYears × (eduQuality − 3) / 2      // university with honours → 1.10
Ktenure = 1 + 0.01 × min(timerecordMonths / 12; 5)        // up to +5% for 5 years at the enterprise

Inflation Coefficient Policy was applied

Automatic monthly salary indexation. It removes conflicts of interest: an employee who feels awkward asking for a raise leaves without warning; one who regularly secures raises spends time on negotiations; salaries at the same level start to depend on bargaining skills.

Kmonth = (1 + annual rate)^(1/12)
Salary for the month = Salary for the previous month × Kmonth
// at a key rate of 14.5% → Kmonth ≈ 1.01135 (+1.13% per month)
  • The benchmark is the Bank of Russia key rate on the date the coefficient is approved; reviewed at least once a year.
  • In financial difficulty indexation may be suspended with advance notice; the salary already reached is not reduced; indexation resumes once things recover.

“Offline Project: Working Together in Person” Policy approved

Remote employees can work together for years without knowing each other in person — and this weakens trust. Once a year, in one of the summer months, a group of engineers gathers in Innopolis for two weeks and works on production tasks together in the open office of the Popov Technopark; the group's senior passes on experience to the juniors.

ProgrammeExample tasks
No. 1 — network engineersSimulating support requests, network demo stands, a charity project (e.g. internet for a village school or museum), teamwork.
No. 2 — electronics, embeddedWorking with an oscilloscope and soldering station, electronic demo stands, a public seminar on automation systems, teamwork.
No. 3 — IoT/IIoT web developersSharing experience in web interfaces, device-monitoring servers, a business-card website for a village museum, teamwork.

Participation is voluntary; the composition and tasks are agreed at least two months in advance. The trip is arranged as a business trip: the enterprise pays for travel and accommodation. Afterwards — a report to management and a participant questionnaire.

“Sport” Programme Policy approved

  • The goal is the physical health of remote employees, who move less without the commute to the office.
  • The employee chooses the sport, time and place. The company pays for 10 hours a month on top of the planned workload, confirmed by a report.
  • The hourly rate is 1% of the monthly salary of a Junior employee. The hours are entered in reports like paid days off.
  • Participation is voluntary, open to all contract types; results are reviewed after six months.

Full text: “Sport” Programme

“HistLit Club” Programme Policy approved

  • Broadens horizons and develops skills that work needs but rarely trains: getting to grips with an unfamiliar subject, selecting sources and briefly presenting conclusions to colleagues; it also gives a distributed team a reason to talk about things other than work.
  • Participants choose a topic and, in their own time, read fiction, non-fiction or history on it, then prepare a talk.
  • Meetings are held no more than once a month and last 45 minutes; each participant is paid for 1 hour.
  • At the end of the year, by participants' vote, speakers receive a 1st, 2nd or 3rd degree bonus: 15, 10 and 5 thousand roubles.

5. Where it is already applied

Rostov Electronics Plant LLC · since 2022

The model serves as the basis of the corporate culture in the distributed R&D team. The policies set out in section 4 are published in Confluence and apply to all employees of the development department.
